Temporal lobe · Lateral cortex

Lateral temporal syndromes

Dominant

  • Wernicke aphasia: fluent, paraphasic jargon; poor comprehension and repetition; often unaware of the deficit and without hemiparesis (inferior MCA division). Easily mistaken for delirium or psychosis [1].
  • Transcortical sensory aphasia: as above but repetition preserved (posterior watershed) [1, 7].
  • Anomic aphasia: inferior / middle temporal gyri [7].
  • Pure word deafness: cannot understand speech, yet reading is intact [1].

Non-dominant

Receptive aprosodia (cannot read emotional tone) [30]; amusia; impaired non-verbal memory; prosopagnosia with fusiform involvement [1].

Bilateral

Cortical deafness or auditory agnosia from bilateral Heschl's gyrus or auditory radiation lesions [1].

References: [1] Adams & Victor · [2] Blumenfeld · [7] Heilman & Valenstein · [30] Ross 1981

Pure word deafness usually needs bilateral superior temporal lesions, or a deep dominant lesion that cuts both auditory radiations off from Wernicke's area. Unilateral Heschl's gyrus lesions cause little deficit because each ear projects to both hemispheres.
